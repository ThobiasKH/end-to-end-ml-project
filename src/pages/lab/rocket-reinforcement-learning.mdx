{/*  */}
{/* ## A small problem with an awkward constraint */}
{/*  */}
{/* The spacecraft cannot choose its velocity or teleport toward the cursor. */}
{/* It can only change **thrust** and **rotation**. To move left, it may first need */}
{/* to turn left. To stop, it may need to turn around and fire the engine again. */}
{/*  */}
{/* Try **Manual** mode to get a feel for this. Then switch to the learned policy, */}
{/* move the target, and compare checkpoints. The untrained network is a real */}
{/* initialization; it is different from the separately labelled random controller. */}
{/*  */}
{/* This is a machine-learning problem because the controller's weights are */}
{/* adjusted from experience. We define the simulation and what counts as a useful */}
{/* outcome. Training searches for a rule that produces those outcomes. */}
{/*  */}
{/* ## What does the agent know? */}
{/*  */}
{/* No images, no mouse events, and no instruction saying “turn left”. */}
{/* The policy receives seven numbers describing the target relative to the craft, */}
{/* its velocity, its heading, and its spin: */}
{/*  */}
{/* $$ */}
{/* s_t = */}
{/* \begin{pmatrix} */}
{/* (g_x-x)/3 \\ */}
{/* (g_y-y)/3 \\ */}
{/* v_x/3 \\ */}
{/* v_y/3 \\ */}
{/* \sin\theta \\ */}
{/* \cos\theta \\ */}
{/* \omega/6 */}
{/* \end{pmatrix}. */}
{/* $$ */}
{/*  */}
{/* All entries are clipped to $[-2,2]$. The live observation display above shows */}
{/* exactly this input, including the normalization. */}
{/*  */}
{/* Relative position makes the same rule useful at different places in the arena. */}
{/* Scaling stops a large velocity from overwhelming the other inputs. */}
{/* The pair $(\sin\theta,\cos\theta)$ avoids an artificial discontinuity: */}
{/* $0$ and $2\pi$ point in the same direction, although their numerical angles */}
{/* look far apart. */}
{/*  */}
{/* The agent does **not** receive the target's velocity. It reacts to the current */}
{/* target position; sudden cursor movements can therefore be much harder than the */}
{/* smooth trajectories used in evaluation. */}
{/*  */}
{/* ## Two numbers come out */}
{/*  */}
{/* The network produces a pair of bounded outputs, $(o_0,o_1)$. We convert them into */}
{/* the engine and turning controls: */}
{/*  */}
{/* $$ */}
{/* T = \frac{o_0+1}{2}, */}
{/* \qquad */}
{/* \tau = o_1. */}
{/* $$ */}
{/*  */}
{/* Thrust $T\in[0,1]$ accelerates along the craft's nose. */}
{/* Torque $\tau\in[-1,1]$ changes its angular velocity. */}
{/* Turning the engine off does not instantly stop the craft. */}
{/*  */}
{/* ## The world underneath the animation */}
{/*  */}
{/* This is a deliberately simplified spacecraft: no gravity, no collisions, */}
{/* and a little damping to make the task manageable. The simulation advances in */}
{/* fixed steps of $\Delta t=1/30$ seconds. Its coordinates have positive $y$ upward, */}
{/* even though the canvas draws downward. World units are labelled as simulated */}
{/* meters. */}
{/*  */}
{/* The order matters. We update spin and heading first, then use the new heading */}
{/* for acceleration: */}
{/*  */}
{/* $$ */}
{/* \begin{aligned} */}
{/* \omega_{t+1} &= 0.94\,\omega_t + 6\Delta t\,\tau_t,\\ */}
{/* \theta_{t+1} &= \theta_t+\Delta t\,\omega_{t+1},\\ */}
{/* v_{t+1} &= 0.99\,v_t+ */}
{/* 4\Delta t\,T_t */}
{/* \begin{pmatrix} */}
{/* \cos\theta_{t+1}\\ */}
{/* \sin\theta_{t+1} */}
{/* \end{pmatrix},\\ */}
{/* p_{t+1} &= p_t+\Delta t\,v_{t+1}. */}
{/* \end{aligned} */}
{/* $$ */}
{/*  */}
{/* The Python training environment and the browser implement the same equations. */}
{/* If either coordinate exceeds five world units in magnitude, the episode ends */}
{/* with an escape penalty. The arena is a training boundary, not a wall that */}
{/* bounces the craft back. */}
{/*  */}
{/* ## What should “good flying” mean? */}
{/*  */}
{/* Reinforcement learning needs a **reward**: a number attached to each action's */}
{/* consequences. This number is part of the design, not something the network */}
{/* discovers for us. */}
{/*  */}
{/* Rewarding proximity alone leaves an awkward loophole. A craft can rush toward */}
{/* the target, pass through it, and sail away. We also want it to slow down nearby. */}
{/* On the other hand, punishing all motion would make doing nothing attractive. */}
{/*  */}
{/* The reward combines progress, distance, arrival speed, and small control costs. */}
{/* Let $d=\lVert p_t-g_t\rVert$ and $d'=\lVert p_{t+1}-g_t\rVert$ be the distances */}
{/* before and after this step, measured against the same target position. */}
{/* Set $q=\exp(-2(d')^2)$: */}
{/*  */}
{/* $$ */}
{/* \begin{aligned} */}
{/* r_t ={}& 4(d-d')-0.10d'\\ */}
{/* &+0.15q-0.08q\lVert v_{t+1}\rVert^2\\ */}
{/* &-0.008T_t-0.004\tau_t^2\\ */}
{/* &-0.002\omega_{t+1}^2. */}
{/* \end{aligned} */}
{/* $$ */}
{/*  */}
{/* The first term rewards getting closer. The second makes lingering far away */}
{/* costly. The $q$ terms reward staying nearby at low speed. The remaining terms */}
{/* discourage excessive thrust, turning, and spin. Escaping adds $-1$. */}
{/*  */}
{/* These weights encode a compromise. A policy can score better on this reward */}
{/* without being the most pleasant controller to watch. That is why we measure */}
{/* tracking error and compare baselines too. */}
{/*  */}
{/* ## What is the neural network actually doing? */}
{/*  */}
{/* The actor is a small multilayer perceptron: */}
{/*  */}
{/* $$ */}
{/* 7\longrightarrow32\longrightarrow32\longrightarrow2. */}
{/* $$ */}
{/*  */}
{/* Each hidden layer multiplies its input by a weight matrix, adds a bias, */}
{/* and applies $\tanh$. A final linear layer produces two latent means; */}
{/* applying $\tanh$ gives the bounded outputs used above. */}
{/*  */}
{/* <details className="rigor-box"> */}
  {/* <summary>Show the entire forward pass</summary> */}
  {/* <div className="rigor-box-body"> */}
{/*  */}
{/* For column-vector inputs, the deterministic policy is */}
{/*  */}
{/* $$ */}
{/* \begin{aligned} */}
{/* h_1 &= \tanh(W_1s+b_1),\\ */}
{/* h_2 &= \tanh(W_2h_1+b_2),\\ */}
{/* \mu &= W_3h_2+b_3,\\ */}
{/* o &= \tanh(\mu). */}
{/* \end{aligned} */}
{/* $$ */}
{/*  */}
{/* The hidden nonlinearity lets the network represent more than one linear */}
{/* transformation. During training, actions are sampled around the latent means */}
{/* to explore. In the browser, we use the mean directly, so a fixed observation */}
{/* and checkpoint always give the same controls. */}
{/*  */}
  {/* </div> */}
{/* </details> */}
{/*  */}
{/* The weights are learned numbers, not a collection of handwritten steering */}
{/* cases. The heuristic controller in the comparison is a separate implementation */}
{/* with explicit position and velocity feedback. */}
{/*  */}
{/* ## Learning by trying things */}
{/*  */}
{/* There is no spreadsheet of correct thrust values. The simulator generates */}
{/* experience: */}
{/*  */}
{/* $$ */}
{/* (s_t,a_t,r_t,s_{t+1}). */}
{/* $$ */}
{/*  */}
{/* We randomize initial position, velocity, heading, spin, and target. Training */}
{/* also includes smooth moving targets. Each rollout supplies examples of what */}
{/* happened after the current policy's actions. */}
{/*  */}
{/* The learning algorithm is **PPO**, implemented in NumPy for this experiment. */}
{/* It collects rollouts and updates the actor using a clipped probability-ratio */}
{/* objective. A separate critic estimates expected future reward; advantage */}
{/* estimates indicate which sampled actions did better than expected. */}
{/* Clipping discourages a single update from changing the policy too aggressively. */}
{/* The algorithm is described in the */}
{/* [original PPO paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347) and */}
{/* [OpenAI's PPO explanation](https://spinningup.openai.com/en/latest/algorithms/ppo.html). */}
{/*  */}
{/* The checkpoint selector and learning curve above come from saved training */}
{/* artifacts. More steps do not guarantee that every checkpoint will behave better. */}
{/* The visitor runs **inference**, not training: moving the cursor changes the */}
{/* input, but never changes the saved weights. */}
{/*  */}
{/* ## Does it work, or does it just look convincing? */}
{/*  */}
{/* Cursor chasing is fun, but it is a poor controlled experiment. The evaluation */}
{/* uses held-out seeded episodes with stationary and smooth moving targets. */}
{/* Every controller faces the same initial conditions and target trajectories. */}
{/* These episodes do not supply training gradients, but repeated progress checks */}
{/* make this a validation suite rather than an untouched final test. */}
{/*  */}
{/* We compare learned checkpoints with an untrained policy, random controls, and */}
{/* a handwritten feedback controller. Lower mean target distance is better; */}
{/* return reflects our chosen reward. Mean thrust records engine use rather than */}
{/* physical fuel consumption. */}
{/*  */}
{/* A “settled” episode means that, over its final 60 steps, mean distance is below */}
{/* $0.45$ world units and mean speed is below $0.8$ units per second. It is a */}
{/* specific test criterion, not proof that the rocket can follow any trajectory. */}
{/* The table above reports the saved measurements rather than estimating them */}
{/* from whatever happens to be on screen. */}
{/* The intermediate learning-curve measurements use 32 episodes, while the */}
{/* checkpoint comparisons use 100; small changes between them need not indicate */}
{/* meaningful improvement. */}
{/*  */}
{/* In the included run, the final learned policy has slightly lower mean tracking */}
{/* error than the feedback controller. The feedback controller uses less thrust */}
{/* and earns higher reward. The learned policy often circles near the target */}
{/* rather than sitting perfectly still. */}
{/*  */}
{/* The goal is to understand what training produced. A single training seed and */}
{/* a simple simulator cannot establish robust real-world flight control. */}
{/*  */}
